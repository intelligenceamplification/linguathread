# English varieties: listening and language awareness

Status: design and audition brief, not a released course or approved voice pack.

## Purpose

Help learners of English and native English speakers hear, understand, and appreciate English as spoken by real communities. English also remains an available meaning anchor. Every speaker has an accent; no variety conveys intelligence, education, or social worth. Treat dialect differences as rule-governed patterns, not errors.

## Initial listening examples

- United States: a West Coast Californian speaker; a New York City speaker; a Southern American speaker identified by their actual region.
- United Kingdom: a British speaker with the specific regional variety identified, not an implied single national accent.
- Australia: an Australian speaker with their variety identified when supported by evidence.

These are points of entry, not comprehensive representations. The South, New York, California, Britain, and Australia each contain substantial internal variation; the Midwest and many other English-speaking communities have distinctive speech as well. Avoid using exaggerated features as a shortcut for authenticity.

## How learners encounter the material

1. Present a short shared meaning or situation and invite learners to listen first. Reveal the transcript after a sound-to-meaning attempt.
2. Replay the selected voice at normal or slow speed, then optionally compare another approved voice in the same situation.
3. Explain only a useful, verified difference in pronunciation, rhythm, vocabulary, grammar, or spelling. Do not assume a spelling difference requires a pronunciation difference or that every speaker uses the same form.
4. Ask for later retrieval through meaning and text, without scoring pronunciation. Preserve separate listening, reading, and writing evidence.
5. Invite comparisons and appreciation for native English speakers as well as learners; do not label a dialect or accent as broken or inferior.

Potential examples for editorial and voice review: American “apartment” and British “flat”; American “elevator” and British “lift”; Australian “holiday” and American “vacation” (both words occur elsewhere, and context matters). For rising intonation on statements, explain that usage and meaning depend on speaker and context; it is not exclusively Californian and should not be prescribed as a regional accent.

## Voice audition and release

Start by auditioning a general American and a specifically identified British teaching voice. Audition Californian, New York, Southern American, and Australian examples in a small comparative set; get the user's listening approval for each voice and accent, including natural pace and short, difficult, and longer material. Do not label synthesized output region-authentic solely from a prompt. Choose samples with guidance from speakers or language specialists where feasible. Exact voices, dialect labels, release order, and allocation are not yet approved.

For each batch, apply the sampling and screening rules in `docs/audio-pipeline.md`. Existing audio and learning records remain intact. Do not imply every example has been recorded or released. The ten-request daily free limit remains in force; a paid build requires explicit authorization. More than one voice for every phrase increases generation and storage costs. Preserve the three-language initial launch scope.

## User-provided listening references

Use these public figures as starting points for researching speaking characteristics and preparing auditions; the descriptions below are the user's impressions, not verified accent classifications or a request to imitate an identifiable individual. Candidates must be original, appropriately licensed voices approved by the user.

- California: David Eagleman; the user is interested in nasal resonance and rising intonation. Do not assume those traits are universal in California.
- New York: Neil deGrasse Tyson as one Black speaker; Larry King and Robert De Niro as additional white speaker references. Race alone does not establish a speaker's dialect or accent; assess each voice and feature on its own evidence.
- Southern United States: Matthew McConaughey; identify the particular regional characteristics under consideration.
- United Kingdom: BBC broadcasters and Zanny Minton Beddoes of The Economist; identify the specific voice and variety rather than treating an institution as one accent.
- Australia: Dr. Alan D. Thompson of The Memo Report; verify the relevant features when choosing a candidate.

The user-provided examples guide future listening research. They do not mean those individuals have endorsed, contributed voices to, or licensed voices for Nuevo Flow. Voice identity, accuracy of regional labels, natural pacing and pronunciation must be assessed in the audition, with no claim of exact personal voice replication.
